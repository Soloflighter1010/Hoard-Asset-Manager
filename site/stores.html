Hoard and your store

How Hoard works with your store, and how it keeps your customers and your platform safe. Hoard is a free, open-source desktop app that VRChat creators use to keep their purchased avatars, outfits and tools in one library on their own computer. This brief explains exactly what it does on your store, what it never does, and how it protects accounts and your servers.

Current as of October 2026 (Hoard 4.x) · Source: github.com/Soloflighter1010/Hoard-Asset-Manager

Each claim links to the code that does it, pinned to commit 19ac106 on the main branch (Hoard 4.3.0), so the line numbers stay put.

It acts as your customer, on their own computer

No accounts, no analytics. Everything runs locally and talks only to the stores the person uses (and, for a set they share as a short link, Hoard's share server, which can't read it).

It never sees a password

People sign in on your own sign-in page. Hoard keeps only the session your site hands out, encrypted by the operating system.

Only what they already own

It reads the person's own library and downloads files their account can already download. Nothing else.

Gentle on your servers

A pause between page loads, one file at a time, and nothing downloaded twice. It doesn't get around bot checks.

What Hoard is

VRChat creators buy assets from several stores and often re-download them for each new Unity project. Hoard gives them one library of everything they own, with local copies they can browse, tag, search and import into Unity through a companion editor package, Hoard for Unity. It also builds a credits list of the creators whose assets a project uses.

Hoard is MIT-licensed and developed in the open on GitHub. It isn't affiliated with, endorsed by or supported by any store, and its documentation says so.

How Hoard uses your store

Every store interaction is started by the person using Hoard, with their own account:

  1. They sign in on your site.

    Hoard opens your normal sign-in page in a browser window that belongs to Hoard alone, separate from their everyday browser. They sign in the usual way (including Google, Discord or X where your site offers it). Hoard never sees or stores the password. Code: browser.py L60–68

  2. Hoard reads their library.

    It opens the same library or inventory pages the customer would, reads the product names, creators, links and pictures, and saves that list locally. Refreshing reads only; it downloads nothing. Code: downloader.py L604–613

  3. They choose what to download.

    Hoard fetches the files from the product's own download buttons or links, as the customer would. Each file is recorded once it's saved, so a later sync only fetches what's new. Code: downloader.py L375

  4. Optional checks for updates.

    On request, or on a schedule the person turns on (off by default, at most every 6 hours), Hoard reads the store again to see whether anything is new or has changed. It asks before downloading any of it. Code: config.py L37

Because Hoard browses as the signed-in customer, its requests carry that session's browser identity: a standard Chrome user agent, with Chrome's "HeadlessChrome" marker replaced by "Chrome". They don't name Hoard. A customer API, with an identifiable client, would change that: see our request below. Code: downloader.py L1397–1400

Shared sets: lists that send buyers to your store

From Hoard 4.2, customers can group products they use together into a set, such as an avatar, its outfits and the shaders they need, and share it as a link. Whoever opens the link sees each product with its picture, its creator and a link to its page on your store. Someone who adds the set to their own Hoard sees which of its products they already own, and Buy on your store for the rest. A set is a list of links back to you: nothing in it is a file, and nothing can be downloaded from it. Code: sets.py L378–382

  • No files, sessions or purchase details. A shared set holds its name, the sharer's note and links to their own photos, and for each product its store, ID, name, creator, public store page and picture address. Never a file, a download link, a sign-in, or what the sharer owns. Their own uploads and anything they've hidden are never in one. Code: server.py L983–994
  • Only public pages, on your own addresses. A product's link is its public page on your website, or its creator's, never a page of the customer's own, such as a purchase's download page or their library. Its picture has to be on your own image host (booth.pximg.net, public-files.gumroad.com, jinxxy-c.jinxxy-cdn.com, images.payhip.com, img.itch.zone). Anything else is dropped, when the set is shared and again when it's opened. Code: sets.py L52–55, sets.py L142–176, set.js L44–62
  • Nothing hosted, nothing collected. The set travels inside the link, after its #, which browsers never send to a server, so Hoard's website never receives it. A customer can instead make a short link (Hoard 4.3), which keeps the set on Hoard's share server for 14 days, locked on their computer first with a key that's only in the link: the server can't read it, and the customer can delete it whenever they like. Code: shared.js L1807–1814, worker.mjs L1–18, server.py L1058–1069 The viewer page can load only its own files, product pictures from the image hosts above, photos from a short list of photo sites and a short link's locked set from the share server, and its Content-Security-Policy enforces that. Code: set.js L1–5, set.html L6
  • Pictures come straight from your image host. The viewer loads each product's picture from your image host, as any page that shows it would, and sends no referrer. In Hoard itself, the picture of a product the customer doesn't own is fetched once, with your store as the referrer, and kept on their computer. If you'd rather your pictures weren't shown this way, tell us. Code: set.js L120–121, library.py L1172–1174
  • Buying stays on your store. Apart from that picture, Hoard reads nothing from your store about products a customer doesn't own. Buy on opens your product page in their everyday browser, where they buy as they always would.

What Hoard never does

  • Touch anything outside the signed-in customer's own purchases, gifts and claimed free items.
  • Get around paywalls, license checks or anything a customer couldn't do in their own browser.
  • Solve or skip a bot check. Where one appears (Payhip shows them), the person completes it themselves in a visible window, and Hoard waits. When Payhip's checks made automated downloading unreliable, Hoard stopped downloading from Payhip altogether. Code: TERMS.md L28–32
  • Ask for, see, or store a password.
  • Send a store's sign-in anywhere but that store. File hosts and CDNs are fetched with a separate session that carries no cookies. Code: egress.py L1–11, egress.py L99
  • Upload, share or redistribute files. Downloads stay on the customer's computer, and Hoard's terms tell people to follow each creator's license and not to share, resell or redistribute. Code: TERMS.md L22–26
  • Collect data. There are no Hoard accounts, analytics, tracking or ads, and nothing readable is sent to the developer: a set a customer shares as a short link is kept on Hoard's share server for 14 days, locked with a key the server never gets.

Protecting your customers' accounts

  • Sessions are encrypted by the operating system: Windows (the user's account), the macOS Keychain, or a Linux keyring. On Linux, Hoard won't save a sign-in at all unless a keyring can protect it. Copying Hoard's files to another computer gives no access. Code: browser.py L65–68
  • Each store is kept apart. Every store has its own sign-in folder and browser profile; one store's pages never share a browser with another store's session. Code: browser.py L73, browser.py L342
  • Signing out is real. Hoard asks the store to end the session where it can (your sign-out button or address), deletes the saved session, checks no copy remains, and removes that store's items from the library so the next person on the computer can't see them. Code: browser.py L814–816
  • Only Hoard can use Hoard. Its local interface needs a key that changes every time it starts, so other programs or other people on a shared computer can't use a signed-in session through it. It's reachable only from the same computer unless the person turns on access from their other devices, which then needs an encrypted connection and the key. Even then, signing in to a store and reading it again only work on the computer running Hoard. Code: safety.py L560
  • Content from stores can't run code. Names, descriptions and pictures are shown as plain text and images. Links labelled with a store only ever open that store's own domain. Code: shared.js L245, safety.py L159–160

Protecting your platform

  • Paced requests. One second between page loads by default. People can only set it between 0.3 and 10 seconds, and Hoard's terms ask them to keep the built-in delays. Code: config.py L21, server.py L329–330
  • One download at a time, resuming an interrupted file where it stopped rather than starting over. A failed file is tried again twice by default, and three times at most, after a short wait. Code: config.py L34, downloader.py L211
  • Nothing fetched twice. Hoard keeps a record of every file it saved, so routine syncs only download what's new or changed. Code: downloader.py L375
  • No background polling by default. Automatic checks are off unless the person turns them on, and then run on the schedule they pick, from every 6 hours to once a month. Code: config.py L37
  • Strict network rules on every request, checked again on each redirect: HTTPS only, public addresses only, and store sessions only sent to that store's own domains. Code: egress.py L1–11, egress.py L122
  • It stops when told no. If a store can't be reached or refuses a request, Hoard reports it and leaves the library as it was. It doesn't hammer the store with retries.

Trustworthy software

  • Open source. Every line is public on GitHub under the MIT license, along with its security policy, privacy policy and terms of use.
  • Built in the open. Releases are built by GitHub Actions from tagged source code, with SHA-256 checksums and GitHub's signed build provenance. The Windows app is code-signed through Microsoft's Trusted Signing. Code: release.yml L89–93, release.yml L155–157, release.yml L231
  • Scanned before release. Every change runs automated tests and CodeQL security analysis, and every release file is scanned by VirusTotal before it's published. Code: check.yml L39, release.yml L11–12
  • Safe updates. Hoard only runs an update installer whose checksum matches the published one. Code: updater.py L9–10, updater.py L200–201
  • Private vulnerability reports. Security problems can be reported privately through GitHub's Security tab, with a first reply within a week. Past reviews and fixes are published in the repository.

What no app can prevent. Hoard can't protect a session from malware already running on the customer's computer, or from someone with administrator access to it. It limits what's exposed: no passwords are kept, each store's session is separate, and copied cookies live only in memory for the length of a download.

Our request: a customer API

Today Hoard has to work through your website as the customer: it reads pages built for people, and keeps a copy of the customer's session to do it. That works, but it's more load on your servers than it needs to be, it breaks when your pages change, and you can't tell Hoard apart from the customer. A small, read-only API for customers would be better for your platform, your customers and the creators who sell with you.

itch.io already offers one, and Hoard uses it: customers make an API key in their itch.io settings, and Hoard reads their library and files through it instead of the website. Code: itch.py L78

What we're asking for

  1. A key the customer controls.

    A personal access token, or OAuth sign-in for apps, that the customer creates in their account settings and can revoke at any time, limited to reading their own purchases and downloading their own files.

  2. Their library.

    Each product they own: its ID, name, creator, store page, picture, and when it was bought or last updated.

  3. A product's files.

    Each file's name, size, version or update date, and a checksum, so apps download only what's new or changed.

  4. Download links.

    A short-lived link for each file, issued only to the owner's key.

  5. Published limits and identification.

    Documented rate limits, and a client ID or user agent each app must send, so you can see which app is calling and block one that misbehaves without affecting your customers.

What your platform gets

  • No customer sessions copied into other apps
  • Far less load: no page rendering, and checksums stop repeat downloads
  • Control: revoke keys, rate-limit per app, see who's calling
  • Nothing breaks when you redesign your site

What Hoard would do

  • Switch to the API as soon as it exists, and stop reading your web pages
  • Ask only for read and download access
  • Identify itself on every request
  • Keep to your published limits

For Jinxxy especially, this would replace the heaviest thing Hoard does today: opening a browser and clicking each download button.

Working together

Hoard aims to be a good citizen on every store it supports. If you'd like it to behave differently on yours, the maintainer would welcome your guidance on:

  • the customer API above, or any other way you'd prefer customers' apps to list their library and fetch their files;
  • request pacing or quiet hours you'd like it to keep;
  • whether Hoard's requests should identify themselves, and how;
  • anything it does today that causes trouble on your side.

Pacing, what Hoard reads and how it fetches files are settings and small code changes, so a request from a store can usually be answered in the next release.

AI disclosure

Hoard was made with a lot of help from AI. Claude, an AI assistant made by Anthropic, wrote most of the code, the visual design and the documentation, working from the maintainer's direction. The maintainer decides what Hoard does, tests it against real store accounts, reports what goes wrong, and publishes the releases.

  • Hoard doesn't use AI while it runs. Nothing about a customer, their library or your store is ever sent to an AI service. AI was only used to build it.
  • AI-written code can contain mistakes, including security mistakes. Every change is tested automatically and scanned by CodeQL, and problems can be reported privately (see Get in touch, above).
  • This document was drafted with Claude too, from Hoard's code and documentation. Each claim links to the code that does it, so you can check it for yourself.
  • Hoard isn't affiliated with or endorsed by Anthropic.
